Think it through

Can a quiz built only from real votes be unfair?

In 2025 the Texas House had 88 Republicans and 62 Democrats. A legislature only votes on bills its majority lets reach the floor. The site’s short quiz uses the session’s best-known bills, and five of its seven are bills Republicans passed over Democratic opposition.

Before 7 October 2026, it had no bills that Democrats passed over Republican opposition. A student who answered “No” to every question was told they “line up with Democrats nearly every time”. A reader noticed, and the site changed it and now explains the lean on the page.

  1. Explain in your own words why a set of real floor votes can lean toward the majority party, even if nobody picked them to be unfair.
  2. The longer quiz uses 67 votes chosen by a published rule that never looks at which party a vote favors. Why might a rule be fairer than a person picking “the important bills”? What could still go wrong with a rule?
  3. Bills that never got a vote cannot appear in any quiz like this. Who decides which bills reach the House floor, and what does that tell you about where power sits in the Legislature?

For the teacher

  • The site is free: no accounts, no ads, no cookies, nothing for students to sign up for. It works on a phone, in English and Spanish (rightnleft.com/es).
  • It endorses no one. Its author donates to the Democratic Party and says so on every page; the vote-selection rule is published and applies the same way to both parties, and each vote links to the official record.
  • Students in the same ZIP may be in different districts. That is real, not an error, and it makes a good aside on how districts are drawn.
  • A quiz result names the party a student’s answers matched (for example “You lean toward Republicans”). Steps 5 and 6 can stay private on the worksheet; skip them if that suits your class better.
  • All 3,546 House floor votes from 2025 are free to download (rightnleft.com/open-data) for a data or statistics extension.
