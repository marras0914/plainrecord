The economy in every presidential term since 1949

Every term here is named, with who held each office. Nothing on this page says a party caused what happened while it held office. The best-known study of this question finds that about half of the difference between the parties comes from things presidents do not control, and cannot explain the rest.

How this is measured, and what it leaves out

Each term, measure by measure

Each term is measured from the quarter after the inauguration to the quarter of the next one, so a term's first quarter counts toward the previous president. That is the convention Blinder and Watson use. They report that every other lag they tried, from none to four quarters, gives a smaller gap between the parties, so the table under each chart shows all five.

* The current term is in progress and is measured through the third quarter of 2026.

Outside shocks

A note for every outside shock Blinder and Watson name, and for every NBER recession after their sample ends in 2012. Chosen by that rule, not by hand. It puts 10 on Republican terms and 5 on Democratic ones, which follows the paper's own finding that Democratic presidents drew milder oil shocks.

  • Truman, 1949 to 1953
    • The Korean War. Blinder and Watson count defense spending due to wars among the factors outside a president's control, and test their result with these terms left out. Source
  • Eisenhower, 1953 to 1957
    • The Korean War. Blinder and Watson count defense spending due to wars among the factors outside a president's control, and test their result with these terms left out. Source
  • Johnson, 1965 to 1969
    • The Vietnam War. Blinder and Watson count defense spending due to wars among the factors outside a president's control, and test their result with these terms left out. Source
  • Nixon, 1969 to 1973
    • The Vietnam War. Blinder and Watson count defense spending due to wars among the factors outside a president's control, and test their result with these terms left out. Source
  • Nixon and Ford, 1973 to 1977
    • The 1973 to 1974 oil shock (OPEC I). Blinder and Watson name it as one major reason growth was slower in this term. Source
  • Carter, 1977 to 1981
    • The 1979 to 1980 oil shock (OPEC II), which Blinder and Watson say helped bring on the high inflation of this term. Source
  • Bush, 1989 to 1993
    • The 1990 to 1991 recession, which Blinder and Watson note is often attributed to tight money and a spike in oil prices. Source
    • The Gulf War. Blinder and Watson count defense spending due to wars among the factors outside a president's control, and test their result with this term left out. Source
  • Bush, 2001 to 2005
    • The wars in Iraq and Afghanistan. Blinder and Watson count defense spending due to wars among the factors outside a president's control, and test their result with these terms left out. Source
  • Bush, 2005 to 2009
    • Oil prices roughly tripled over this term, which Blinder and Watson say played an important role in the onset of the Great Recession. Source
    • The financial crisis of late 2008. NBER dates the recession around it from December 2007 to June 2009. Source
    • The wars in Iraq and Afghanistan. Blinder and Watson count defense spending due to wars among the factors outside a president's control, and test their result with these terms left out. Source
  • Obama, 2009 to 2013
    • The financial crisis of late 2008. NBER dates the recession around it from December 2007 to June 2009. Source
    • The wars in Iraq and Afghanistan. Blinder and Watson count defense spending due to wars among the factors outside a president's control, and test their result with these terms left out. Source
  • Trump, 2017 to 2021
    • The pandemic of early 2020. NBER dates a recession from February to April 2020, and notes the economy contracted so sharply in March that the first quarter fell well below the last of 2019. Source
